# Over-diversification

Over-diversification is the point at which adding more holdings stops reducing risk in any meaningful way and starts creating drawbacks instead. Diversification has diminishing returns: the first several uncorrelated holdings cut risk sharply, but each additional one matters less. Past a certain breadth, you gain almost no extra protection while taking on more cost, complexity, and dilution.

## Diminishing returns

Most of the risk reduction from diversification arrives early. Moving from one holding to ten dramatically lowers specific risk; moving from a hundred to two hundred barely changes it. After enough holdings, the only risk left is the market risk that diversification cannot touch, so adding more does nothing about it. There is a practical range beyond which extra positions are mostly redundant, each one too small to help and too similar to differ.

## The downsides of too much

Spreading too thin brings real costs. More holdings can mean more trading fees, more funds with their own expenses, and far more to track and rebalance. It can also dilute your best ideas: a strong holding becomes a tiny sliver that barely moves the portfolio. And buying so many investments that you effectively own the whole market, while paying active prices for it, means paying extra for something a single low-cost index fund delivers more cheaply.

## The sensible middle

The goal is enough diversification to remove most specific risk without piling on redundant positions. For many investors, a handful of broad, low-cost funds covering different asset classes and regions achieves wide diversification simply and cheaply, without owning hundreds of individual holdings directly. The aim is genuine breadth across different kinds of risk, not the largest possible count of line items. More holdings are not automatically safer; better-chosen, less-correlated holdings are.
