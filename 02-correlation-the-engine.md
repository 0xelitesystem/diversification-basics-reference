# Correlation, the engine

Correlation is what actually makes diversification work: the benefit comes not from owning many things, but from owning things that do not move in lockstep. Correlation measures how two investments tend to move relative to each other. The lower the correlation between your holdings, the more their individual ups and downs cancel out, and the smoother your combined return becomes.

## What correlation means

Correlation runs from positive one to negative one. Two investments with a correlation near positive one move together: when one rises, the other tends to rise too. A correlation near negative one means they move in opposite directions. A correlation near zero means their movements are largely unrelated. Diversification benefits most when correlations are low or negative, because that is when one holding is likely to be steady or rising while another falls.

## Why count alone is not enough

Owning fifty stocks sounds diversified, but if all fifty are large technology companies, they will tend to move together and share the same risks. Their high correlation means a blow to that sector hits them all at once, so the portfolio behaves much like a single concentrated bet. True diversification depends on the relationships between holdings, not the raw number of them. A smaller set of genuinely uncorrelated assets can be better diversified than a large set of similar ones.

## Correlations can shift

A practical caution is that correlations are not fixed. Assets that normally move independently can suddenly move together during a crisis, when fear drives investors to sell almost everything at once. This is why diversification can feel weakest exactly when it is most wanted. The relationships hold most of the time and still provide real protection, but treating any correlation as permanent is a mistake. Diversification is a strong tool, not an iron guarantee.
