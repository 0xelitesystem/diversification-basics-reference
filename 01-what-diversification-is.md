# What diversification is

Diversification is the practice of spreading your money across many different investments so that no single holding can determine the fate of the whole portfolio. The idea is simple: if you own many things that do not all rise and fall at the same time, a bad outcome in one is cushioned by the others. It reduces the risk tied to any individual investment without requiring you to predict which one will do best.

## Why it lowers risk

Any single investment carries risk specific to it: a company can mismanage itself, an industry can fall out of favor, a country can hit trouble. This is called specific or idiosyncratic risk. When you hold only one or a few investments, that specific risk is concentrated, and one bad event can be devastating. Spreading across many holdings means a problem in one affects only a small slice of the total, so the portfolio as a whole is far steadier than its individual parts.

## What it does not remove

Diversification reduces the risk that is unique to individual investments, but it cannot remove the risk shared by the whole market. A broad downturn, a recession, or a systemic shock can push almost everything down at once. This shared risk is called market or systematic risk, and no amount of diversifying within an asset class eliminates it. Understanding this limit is important: diversification smooths the bumps caused by individual holdings, not the larger tides that move everything together.

## A concrete picture

Imagine putting everything into one company's stock. If that company fails, you lose nearly all of it. Now imagine spreading the same money across hundreds of companies. For your portfolio to be wiped out, hundreds would have to fail at once, which is far less likely than one failing. The expected return can stay similar, but the range of outcomes narrows. You give up the chance of one lucky pick making you rich in exchange for protection against one unlucky pick ruining you, a trade most investors are glad to make.
